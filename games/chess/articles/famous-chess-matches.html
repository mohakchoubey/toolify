Famous Grandmaster Matches: 8 Games That Changed Chess Forever

By Toolify Editorial • 11 min read • Updated: Sep 28, 2026 • ✨ 100% Ad-Free Experience

Some chess games are remembered for a single brilliant sacrifice. Others are remembered because of the players, the rivalry, or what the game meant for the world outside the board. Studying these classic battles is one of the most enjoyable ways to improve, because they turn abstract ideas into memorable stories.

A quick note before we begin: the official Grandmaster title only became formal in the 20th century, so a few of the players below lived before it existed. They are included here because their games shaped how the great players of today think. If you are new to the game, it helps to read What is Chess? first, and to know how each piece moves from our rules guide.

8 Games and Matches That Changed Chess

1. The Immortal Game

1851 · London · Anderssen vs Kieseritzky

Adolf Anderssen sacrificed a bishop, both rooks, and even his queen, and still checkmated his opponent with his remaining minor pieces. It was played in a casual setting rather than a tournament, yet it became the ultimate example of the romantic style of chess, where beauty and attack mattered more than material.

2. The Opera Game

1858 · Paris · Morphy vs the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard

Paul Morphy played this game against two aristocrats who were consulting together while watching a performance at the opera. Morphy developed his pieces rapidly, opened lines toward the enemy king, and finished with a queen sacrifice followed by checkmate. It is still used to teach development, activity, and initiative.

3. The Game of the Century

1956 · New York · Donald Byrne vs Bobby Fischer

A 13-year-old Bobby Fischer defeated a strong opponent with a stunning queen sacrifice, the shock move 17...Be6!!, and then won in 41 moves. The game announced that a new prodigy had arrived, and it remains one of the most celebrated games ever played by a teenager.

4. Fischer vs Spassky, World Championship

1972 · Reykjavik · Match of the Century

The American challenger Bobby Fischer faced the Soviet champion Boris Spassky in a match watched around the world during the Cold War. Fischer won 12½–8½, becoming the first American-born world champion and ending a long period of Soviet dominance. Game 6, where Fischer opened with 1. c4 and played a superb positional game, earned applause even from Spassky.

5. Karpov vs Kasparov

1984–1985 · Moscow

The first match between Anatoly Karpov and Garry Kasparov lasted 48 games before it was stopped without a winner. A new match followed in 1985, and Kasparov won it at the age of 22, becoming the youngest world champion at that time. Their rivalry, with Karpov's positional control against Kasparov's dynamic energy, dominated chess for years.

6. Kasparov vs Deep Blue

1996–1997 · New York · Man vs Machine

Kasparov beat IBM's Deep Blue in 1996, but in the 1997 rematch the machine won. Game 6 ended in just 19 moves and it was the first time a computer defeated a reigning world champion in a match. The result made headlines around the world and marked a turning point in the relationship between humans and computers.

7. Kasparov vs Topalov

1999 · Wijk aan Zee · Kasparov's Immortal

Garry Kasparov played a spectacular rook sacrifice on d4 and launched an attack that dragged the opponent's king across the board. Many players consider it one of the finest attacking games of the modern era, and it proves that great chess is not only about old masters.

8. AlphaZero vs Stockfish

2017 · Artificial Intelligence

DeepMind's neural-network program AlphaZero taught itself chess by playing against itself and then faced Stockfish, one of the strongest traditional engines. In a 100-game match it won 28 games and drew 72 without losing. Its bold style, including long-term sacrifices for activity, inspired human players to rethink some ideas.

The Modern Era

Magnus Carlsen dominated the 2010s and held the world title from 2013 to 2023, helping chess grow online. In 2024 the young Indian player D. Gukesh won the World Championship at 18, becoming the youngest undisputed champion in history. Today chess engines are far stronger than any human, yet the game is more popular than ever.

What Beginners Can Learn From These Games

  • Development wins games. The Opera Game shows what happens when one side has all its pieces active and the other does not. See our beginner openings for how to get there.
  • Initiative matters. Keeping your opponent busy with threats can be stronger than winning material.
  • Sacrifices need a reason. Every famous sacrifice above was backed by concrete calculation, not hope.
  • Never stop learning. Even world champions study the games of others, and today they also study engines.
