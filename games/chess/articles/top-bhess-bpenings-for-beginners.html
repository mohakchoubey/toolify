Top Chess Openings for Beginners: 7 Simple Setups to Start With

By Toolify Editorial • 10 min read • Updated: Sep 28, 2026 • ✨ 100% Ad-Free Experience

The opening is the first ten to fifteen moves of a chess game, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. A good opening gives your pieces room to work, keeps your king safe, and puts you in a comfortable position to fight in the middlegame.

The good news for beginners is that you do not need to memorize long lines of theory. It is much more valuable to understand why the moves are played. If you are new to the game, start with our beginner's guide to chess and the basic rules and movements, then come back here to build your first repertoire.

Three Golden Opening Principles

1. Fight for the Center

The four central squares (d4, d5, e4, and e5) are the most important on the board. Pieces placed in or aiming at the center control more squares and can reach either side of the board quickly. That is why most openings begin with a pawn move such as e4 or d4.

2. Develop Your Pieces Quickly

Bring your knights and bishops out early, and usually develop knights before bishops. Pieces that stay on the back rank are not helping you. Try to move each piece once and put it on an active square rather than moving the same piece again and again.

3. Castle Early

Castling tucks your king into a safer corner and brings a rook toward the center. Aim to castle within your first ten moves. A king stuck in the middle of an open board is a target.

What to avoid: bringing your queen out too early, making too many pawn moves, moving the same piece twice without a reason, and grabbing a pawn while your pieces are still undeveloped.

Best Openings for White

1. Italian Game

Beginner friendlyOpen play
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4

The Italian Game follows the principles almost perfectly: a pawn in the center, a knight developed, and a bishop aimed at the sensitive f7 square. You will quickly learn about castling, controlling the center, and basic tactics. It is popular at every level, from beginners to grandmasters.

2. London System

Beginner friendlyLow theory
1. d4 d5 2. Nf3 Nf6 3. Bf4

The London System is called a system opening because you can play a very similar setup against almost any reply. The bishop goes to f4, pawns to e3 and c3, and the pieces develop around them. It is solid and easy to remember, although it is worth learning other openings as well so your understanding does not become too narrow.

3. Queen's Gambit

ClassicStrategic
1. d4 d5 2. c4

White offers a wing pawn to challenge Black's central pawn. If Black accepts, White usually wins the pawn back and gains a strong center. If Black declines, a rich strategic game begins. It has been played for centuries, and it opens the door to the whole world of 1. d4 chess.

4. Scotch Game

AggressiveOpen play
1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. d4

Instead of slowly preparing the center, the Scotch Game challenges it right away. The position opens quickly, which gives beginners a lot of chances to practice tactics and quick piece activity.

Best Defenses for Black

5. Caro-Kann Defense

SolidBeginner friendly
1. e4 c6

Black prepares to challenge the center with ...d5 while keeping a very sturdy pawn structure. The Caro-Kann is respected for being safe and reliable, and the positions are easy to understand.

6. Slav Defense

SolidReliable
1. d4 d5 2. c4 c6

The Slav is a trusted answer to the Queen's Gambit. Black supports the d5 pawn with a pawn on c6 and keeps the position healthy, so White has to work hard to gain any advantage.

7. Scandinavian Defense

DirectSimple plan
1. e4 d5

Black challenges White's e4 pawn immediately. After 2. exd5 Qxd5 the queen comes out early, which is normally something to avoid, but in this opening it is a well-known plan with a clear structure. It is simple to learn and does not need a lot of memorization.

Once you feel comfortable, you can explore deeper openings such as the Ruy Lopez (1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5) or the Sicilian Defense (1. e4 c5). They are extremely popular, but they also involve a lot more theory.

"When you see a good move, look for a better one." — attributed to Emanuel Lasker

Common Opening Mistakes

  • Memorizing without understanding. If your opponent leaves your memorized line, you will not know what to do. Learn the ideas behind the moves.
  • Ignoring your opponent's threats. After every move, ask what your opponent is threatening now.
  • Neglecting the king. Delaying castling can lead to quick attacks.
  • Trying too many openings. Pick one plan as White and one or two replies as Black, then play them repeatedly.

How to Practice Your Openings

The best way to learn an opening is to play it. Choose one setup for White and one for Black, play them for a few weeks, and review your games to see where you went wrong. Try them out in a game of CheckMate against a friend or the computer. Over time you will start to recognize patterns automatically. It also helps to study how great players handled the early moves in the famous grandmaster matches we have covered.

Conclusion: Start Simple, Play Often

You do not need a huge repertoire to enjoy chess. A simple opening for White, a solid reply for Black, and a firm understanding of center control, development, and king safety are enough to carry you a long way.

Pick an opening from this list, play it in your next few games, and pay attention to what you learn. Improvement comes from playing, reviewing, and repeating.
