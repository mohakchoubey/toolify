What is Chess? A Beginner's Guide to the World's Favorite Strategy Game

By Toolify Editorial • 8 min read • Updated: Sep 28, 2026 • ✨ 100% Ad-Free Experience
📚 All Chess Articles 📖 What is Chess? A Beginner's Guide Current Page ⚔️ History of Chess 📜 Basic Rules & Movements Explained ♟ Benefits of Playing Chess 🧠 Top Chess Openings for Beginners 🏆 Famous Grandmaster Matches

Chess is a two-player strategy board game played on a square board of 64 alternating light and dark squares. There are no dice, no cards, and no hidden information. Every piece is visible, every rule is the same for both players, and the result depends entirely on the decisions you make.

The game has been played for well over a thousand years and today it is enjoyed by millions of people, from children in school clubs to world champions competing for global titles. If you have never touched a chess piece, this guide explains what chess is, how the game works, and how to take your first steps.

Chess at a Glance

1. A Game for Two Players

One player controls the white pieces and the other controls the black pieces. White always moves first, and then the players take turns making exactly one move each. You cannot skip a turn, and you cannot pass.

2. The Chessboard

The board is an 8 by 8 grid. The columns are called files (labeled a to h) and the rows are called ranks (numbered 1 to 8). Every square therefore has its own name, such as e4 or g8. When you set up the board, each player should have a light square in their right-hand corner.

3. The Pieces

Each player starts with 16 pieces, making 32 on the board: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. Every type of piece moves in its own way, and learning those movements is the first real step in chess. You can study them in detail in our guide to basic rules and movements.

4. The Goal: Checkmate

The goal of chess is not to capture the most pieces. It is to checkmate the opponent's king. A king is in check when it is under attack. If it is in check and there is no legal way to escape, that is checkmate and the game is over. Capturing pieces is only useful because it helps you reach that goal.

5. The Three Phases of a Game

Most games move through three stages. In the opening, both players develop their pieces, fight for the center, and get their kings to safety. In the middlegame, plans, tactics, and attacks decide who gains an advantage. In the endgame, fewer pieces remain, kings become active, and pawns often race to promotion. Our list of top chess openings for beginners is a great place to start with the first phase.

6. How a Game Can End

Checkmate is the most famous result, but it is not the only one. A game can also finish when a player resigns, when a player runs out of time on the clock, or in a draw. Draws happen by agreement, by stalemate (a player has no legal move but is not in check), by threefold repetition of a position, by the fifty-move rule, or when neither side has enough material left to checkmate.

7. Time Controls

Chess is often played with a clock. In general, bullet games are the fastest, blitz games last a few minutes per side, rapid games give more time to think, and classical games can last for hours. Beginners usually enjoy rapid games because there is time to think without the pressure of a ticking clock.

8. Notation and Ratings

Moves are recorded in algebraic notation. For example, e4 means a pawn moves to the e4 square and Nf3 means a knight moves to f3. Players also have a rating, based on the Elo system, that estimates their strength. The international governing body, FIDE, awards titles such as FIDE Master and Grandmaster.

"Chess is the gymnasium of the mind." — attributed to Blaise Pascal

Why Is Chess So Popular?

Chess is easy to learn but almost impossible to master. The rules fit on a single page, yet the number of possible games is so large that no two serious games are ever exactly alike. There is no luck involved, so every win is earned and every loss is a chance to learn.

It is also accessible. All you need is a board, or a phone, or a browser. You can play a friend across the table, a stranger on the other side of the world, or a computer at any difficulty level. And because chess rewards patience, planning, and focus, many parents and teachers encourage it as a healthy mental activity. You can read more about that in our article on the benefits of playing chess.

How to Start Playing Chess

  • Learn how each piece moves. Start with the pawn, rook, bishop, knight, queen, and king. Practice one piece at a time.
  • Learn the special moves. Castling, en passant, and pawn promotion look strange at first but are easy after a few games.
  • Follow the opening principles. Control the center, develop your knights and bishops, and castle early.
  • Play real games. Nothing teaches faster than practice. Jump into a game of CheckMate and try to play a full game from start to finish.
  • Review your mistakes. After each game, ask yourself where the game changed. This habit improves players faster than anything else.
  • Learn from the greats. Studying classic games such as those in our famous grandmaster matches article shows how ideas turn into victories.
New to the game? Do not worry about memorizing everything on day one. Play, lose a few games, and learn from them. Every strong player started exactly where you are now.
