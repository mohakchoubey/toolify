Basic Rules & Movements in Chess: A Beginner's Guide

By Toolify Editorial • 10 min read • Updated: Sep 27, 2026 • ✨ 100% Ad-Free Experience

Chess is a two-player strategy game played on an 8×8 board containing 64 squares. Each player begins with 16 pieces, including a king, queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights, and eight pawns. The objective is to checkmate the opponent's king while protecting your own.

Learning chess becomes much easier once you understand how each piece moves, how captures work, and the basic rules that control the game. This guide explains the essential rules and movements you need to start playing chess confidently.

Basic Rules of Chess

1. The Chessboard

A chessboard contains 64 alternating light and dark squares arranged in eight rows and eight columns. Each player should have a light-colored square in the bottom-right corner when the board is positioned correctly.

The horizontal rows are called ranks and are numbered 1 through 8. The vertical columns are called files and are labeled a through h. Every square therefore has a unique coordinate, such as e4, d5, or h1.

2. Setting Up the Pieces

Each player starts with eight pieces and eight pawns. The back rank is arranged with the rook, knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight, and rook.

The queen is placed on a square matching her color: the white queen starts on a light square and the black queen starts on a dark square. The eight pawns occupy the rank directly in front of the other pieces.

3. White Moves First

In a standard chess game, White always makes the first move. Players then alternate turns, making one legal move at a time.

4. Capturing Pieces

A piece captures an opponent's piece by moving to the square occupied by that piece, provided the move is legal. The captured piece is removed from the board.

Except for the pawn, most pieces capture in the same basic pattern that they use for normal movement.

How Each Chess Piece Moves

5. The King

The king is the most important piece in chess. It can move one square in any direction: horizontally, vertically, or diagonally.

A king cannot move onto a square where it could be captured by an opponent's piece. Because losing the king is the central danger of the game, players must always keep their king safe.

6. The Queen

The queen is the most mobile piece on the board. It can move any number of squares horizontally, vertically, or diagonally, as long as no other piece blocks its path.

Because the queen combines the movement of a rook and a bishop, it can control many squares at once and is especially powerful in open positions.

7. The Rook

A rook can move any number of squares horizontally or vertically. It cannot jump over another piece.

Rooks become particularly useful when files and ranks are open because they can travel long distances across the board.

8. The Bishop

A bishop moves diagonally any number of squares as long as its path is clear. Each bishop remains on the same color of square for the entire game.

This means that a bishop that starts on a light square will always remain on light squares, while a bishop that starts on a dark square will always remain on dark squares.

9. The Knight

The knight has a unique movement pattern. It moves in an L-shape: two squares in one direction and then one square perpendicular to that direction, or one square followed by two.

The knight is the only standard chess piece that can jump over other pieces. This makes it especially useful when the board is crowded.

10. The Pawn

Pawns have the most unusual movement rules. A pawn normally moves one square forward. From its starting position, it may move two squares forward if both squares are clear.

Pawns capture diagonally one square forward. They cannot capture pieces directly in front of them.

When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the board, it must be promoted. It can usually become a queen, rook, bishop, or knight.

Special Chess Moves

11. Castling

Castling is a special move involving the king and one rook. The king moves two squares toward the rook, and the rook moves to the square immediately on the other side of the king.

Castling is only legal when the king and the chosen rook have not previously moved, the squares between them are empty, and the king is not currently in check or moving through a square controlled by an opponent's piece.

12. En Passant

En passant is a special pawn capture. If an opponent's pawn moves two squares forward from its starting position and lands beside your pawn, your pawn may capture it as if it had moved only one square.

This capture must be made immediately on the next move; otherwise, the opportunity is lost.

13. Pawn Promotion

When a pawn reaches the final rank on the opponent's side of the board, it is promoted. The player can choose a queen, rook, bishop, or knight.

Promotion can dramatically change the position because a pawn can become a powerful new piece.

Check, Checkmate, and the Goal of Chess

14. What Is Check?

A king is in check when an opponent's piece is attacking its square. The player whose king is in check must respond immediately by moving the king, capturing the attacking piece, or blocking the attack when possible.

15. What Is Checkmate?

Checkmate occurs when a king is in check and there is no legal move that can remove the threat. Checkmate ends the game.

16. What Is Stalemate?

Stalemate occurs when a player has no legal move available, but their king is not in check. A stalemate results in a draw.

Important Rules Beginners Should Remember

  • White always moves first.
  • Players alternate turns.
  • You cannot make a move that leaves your own king in check.
  • A piece generally cannot move through another piece unless its movement specifically allows it.
  • The knight can jump over pieces.
  • Pawns move forward but capture diagonally.
  • The king cannot move onto a square controlled by an opponent's piece.
  • The game ends when a player is checkmated, resigns, or a draw is reached under the applicable rules.

Understanding Piece Values

Chess pieces are often given approximate point values to help beginners understand exchanges. A commonly used guide is:

  • Pawn: 1 point
  • Knight: 3 points
  • Bishop: 3 points
  • Rook: 5 points
  • Queen: 9 points
  • King: No conventional point value because it cannot be captured

These values are useful guidelines rather than absolute rules. The actual strength of a piece depends on the position, activity, threats, and many other factors.

Basic Chess Strategy for Beginners

17. Control the Center

The central squares are important because pieces placed near the center can often influence more areas of the board. Beginners should understand why moves involving central pawns and pieces are common in the opening.

18. Develop Your Pieces

Development means bringing your knights, bishops, and other pieces into useful positions. Avoid moving the same piece repeatedly in the opening without a clear reason.

19. Protect Your King

King safety is one of the most important priorities in chess. Castling is a common way to move the king to relative safety while bringing a rook toward the center.

20. Think Before Every Move

Before making a move, check whether your opponent has a capture, check, or immediate threat. A simple habit of looking for your opponent's response can prevent many beginner mistakes.

"Chess is a game of mistakes. The player who makes the next-to-last mistake wins."

Conclusion: Learn the Rules, Then Make Your Move

Understanding how the chess pieces move is the foundation of becoming a better chess player. Once you know the basic rules, special moves, check, checkmate, and draw conditions, you can begin focusing on tactics, strategy, openings, and endgames.

Chess becomes more interesting as you learn to combine individual piece movements into complete plans. Start with the fundamentals, practice regularly, and gradually develop your ability to see threats and opportunities across the board.
